## Overview

This is a module about orders, which deals with exponents in NT. 

## Definition 

Let's define orders first. 

*The **order** of $a\pmod p$, sometimes written as $\text{ord}_p (a)$, is the **smallest** positive integer $m$ such that $a^m\equiv 1\pmod p$.* 

The idea is quite similar to the logarithm function, but in NT.

## Fermat's Little Theorem

First, you may question how do you know this value exists. After all, what if, for all integers $m$, $a^m \not\equiv 1 \pmod p$? To do this, we must first show the famous Fermat's Little Theorem.

** Fermat's Little Theorem ** $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$ for all integers $a$ and primes $p$ where $\gcd(a,p) = 1$.

To prove this, consider two sets $A = \{1,2,\ldots, p-1\}$ and $B = \{a\cdot 1, a\cdot 2,\ldots, a\cdot (p-1)\}$. We will show all elements of $B$ are distinct. Consider two elements $\epsilon_1,\epsilon_2 \in A$. For the sake of contradiction, suppose $a\cdot \epsilon_1$ and $a\cdot \epsilon_2$ are the same. Then,
$$a\cdot \epsilon_1 - a\cdot \epsilon_2 \equiv a(\epsilon_1 - \epsilon_2) \equiv 0 \pmod p. $$
As $\gcd(a,p) = 1$, then $\epsilon_1 - \epsilon_2 \equiv 0 \pmod p$, and so $\epsilon_1 \equiv \epsilon_2 \pmod p$. But, $0 \leq \epsilon_1, \epsilon_2 \leq p-1$, impossible, unless $\epsilon_1 = \epsilon_2$. Hence, all elements of $B$ are distinct. Nicely enough, we can multiply the elements of $A$ and $B$, and upon taking the results modulo $p$, they must be equal:

$$ \prod A \equiv \prod B \implies (p-1)! \equiv a^{p-1} \cdot (p-1)! \pmod p \implies (p-1)! \cdot \left(a^{p-1}-1\right) \equiv 0 \pmod p.$$

Note that $p$ is relatively prime to all elements of $A$, and so $\gcd(p, (p-1)!) = 1$. So, $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, as desired.

## minimality 

The power of orders is its minimality. To be more precise: 

*Theorem 1. If $m=\text{ord}_p (a)$, and $k$ is a nonnegative integer such that $a^k\equiv 1\pmod p$, then $m\mid k$.*

*proof: The useful idea in orders is that you can take modulo on exponents. 
Suppose $k=mq+r$ where $0<r<m$, then we have $a^k\equiv 1\pmod p\iff a^r\equiv 1\pmod p$, which contradicts the minimality of $m$, as needed.*

Some students might also notice that: 

*problem: Prove that $\text{ord}_p (a)\mid \phi (p)$. In particular, if $p$ is a prime then $\text{ord}_p (a)\mid p-1$. (Hint: use Euler's theorem directly.)*

Thus the minimality of orders can be powerful if you know other exponents $k$ with $a^k\equiv 1\pmod p$. 

## primitive roots 

The converse also holds for a prime: There **exists** an integer $a$ such that $\text{ord}_p (a)=p-1$. For example: 

**problem**: Check that $2$ is a primitive root both $\pmod {11}$ and $\pmod {13}$. 

Primitive root always **exists** for a prime $p$, often denoted as $g$, but it's not always true that we know the exact value of $g$.
